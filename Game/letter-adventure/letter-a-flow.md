# Letter Adventure: Letter A Flow

## Purpose

Sketch the first short browser-game experience: help a pre-K learner notice uppercase **A** and lowercase **a**, connect **A** with **apple**, and practice choosing the target letter. This is an early design draft for discussion and a small child-and-caregiver tryout, not a finished interface specification.

## Session at a glance

- **Setting:** a simple, colorful two-dimensional adventure in a desktop browser.
- **Target:** recognize uppercase A and lowercase a, then connect A with the beginning of *apple*.
- **Session length:** about 2–3 minutes.
- **Input:** large click-or-tap choices; no keyboard precision required.
- **Tone:** warm, calm, and encouraging. A wrong choice is part of trying, not a penalty.

## Flow

| Beat | On screen / instruction | Child action | Feedback and next attempt |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Meet A** | A friendly guide and an apple appear beside large **A a** letters. The guide says: “Let’s find the letter A. A says /a/, like apple.” | Look, listen, and optionally point to or say the letter with a caregiver. | The letter and apple remain visible as reference. Continue when the child is ready. |
| **2. Find uppercase A** | Prompt: “Can you find A?” Show three large letter choices, including uppercase A and two familiar distractors. | Click or tap one letter. | **If correct:** the guide celebrates briefly and moves along the trail: “You found A!” Continue to the path. **If incorrect:** the choice gently returns to its place; say, “That one is [letter]. Let’s look for A.” Keep the choices available and invite another try. |
| **3. Follow the A trail** | A short, uncluttered path shows several letter markers, including five target A markers and a few distractors. Prompt: “Choose the A letters to help me follow the trail.” | Click or tap an A marker at a time. | **If correct:** the character moves one step and the marker gives a brief visual response. **If incorrect:** the character stays in place; identify the selected letter gently and invite the child to try another marker. If the child hesitates, highlight the A shape as a cue. |
| **4. Connect A to apple** | Show the apple and ask: “Which letter does apple start with?” Offer uppercase A among a small set of choices; keep lowercase a visible nearby as a related form. | Choose the letter. The child may also name the picture or letter, but speaking is optional. | **If correct:** “Apple starts with A!” The guide reaches the apple. **If incorrect:** repeat the word slowly, emphasize its first sound, and offer another try with the A reference visible. |
| **5. Finish and choose** | The guide reaches the apple. Show a short celebration and two clear options: “Play A again” and “Try another letter.” | Choose whether to repeat or stop. | End on success and leave the next step clear. No score, timer, or failure screen. |

## Feedback rules

- Respond immediately to each choice so the child can see what happened.
- Name the target letter and, when useful, the selected distractor; keep language brief.
- Treat an incorrect choice as information: offer a cue, preserve the chance to try again, and avoid taking away progress already earned.
- Keep the child moving toward the same learning goal while reducing difficulty when needed (for example, show fewer choices or highlight the target shape).
- Allow a caregiver to read prompts aloud. Audio narration can be added, but the first design should remain understandable with visible prompts and pictures.

## What to learn in a first tryout

Observe whether the child can understand the prompt, make a choice without fine-motor difficulty, and use the feedback to attempt the task again. Ask the caregiver where they needed to explain or intervene, and whether the session felt short and clear. Use those observations to revise the prompts, choice count, and cues before adding more letters or game features.
